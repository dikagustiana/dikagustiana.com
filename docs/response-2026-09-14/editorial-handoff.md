**Established, runtime-verified on 14 September 2026**: all 165 essay records were searched

The site now carries a placeholder on About stating only the supplied fact, labelled as a
recollection, with the four missing pieces named. That is the most that can be published
without invention.

**What only you can supply.** Four paragraphs, roughly 100–200 words each:

1. **The original argument, as you actually made it.** Not "I wanted a high carbon tax" but
   the reasoning: what you thought the tax would do, through what mechanism, and why you
   thought the level mattered more than anything else. Without this there is nothing to
   compare the revision against.
2. **The assumption that failed.** One sentence naming the thing that turned out to be
   wrong. A change of position is only informative if it names this.
3. **The evidence or experience that was decisive.** If it was a specific project, a
   specific document, or a specific conversation, say which. If it was gradual, say that —
   "gradual" is an honest answer and "a revelation" would not be.
4. **What remains uncertain, and what could change it again.** A revised position stated
   without its own failure conditions has learned the wrong lesson from the first one.

**Two constraints the draft must hold.** The undergraduate argument is a recollection unless
you have the document; if you do not, the piece should say so. And the revision must not be
an equally unqualified "infrastructure always first" — the position your written work
actually supports is the narrowed one already quoted on About: *"Infrastructure is a
prerequisite when clean firm power, transmission, or emissions data is the binding
constraint. It is not universally first."*

**Suggested genre**: `argued-position` if you take a view on carbon pricing now;
`exploratory` if the reversal is still settling. Either way it needs a source.

## 2. Run the comparative test on one named corridor (Findings 3, A04)

This is step 5 of the reading path and is currently shown as a gap.

The flagship essay states the test — which of capacity, access, contract terms and risk
allocation actually binds — and says explicitly that the parameters to answer it "have not
been assembled in the public literature, even where the underlying data exist."

**What would turn the test into a finding.** One corridor or one industrial estate. One
outcome, one horizon. For that place, four separate questions answered separately:

| Constraint | The question |
| --- | --- |
| Capacity | Does the clean firm power exist? |
| Network | Can the network carry it to the plant gate? |
| Contract | Do existing terms — take-or-pay, dispatch priority, tenor — prevent the change even where the power exists? |
| Risk allocation | Is it bankable, and if not, is that a coverage-timing problem or a returns problem? |

**Hold this open**: the IEA found in 2022 that Java-Bali and Sumatra could accommodate a 10%
solar share in a 2025 scenario through operating changes, with contractual inflexibility the
live problem. That is the strongest available reason not to assume the answer is capacity.

Until this exists, the site's thesis is a framing, not a finding, and the reading path says
so.

## 5. The employment trade-off (Finding 8)

`SHIFT_BY_ID.reindustrialisation.criterion.unresolved` states the question and does not
answer it, because answering it would be inventing a moral priority:

> Where the two objectives conflict — a configuration with more near-term jobs and higher
> emissions or higher public cost, against a cleaner one with fewer — which constraint
> governs?

Three candidate forms, any of which would be a decision:

1. **An emissions ceiling** — no configuration above X, whatever its employment.
2. **A durable-jobs floor** — no support below Y durable jobs, whatever its emissions.
3. **A public cost per durable job cap** — the criterion that makes the two comparable, and
   the hardest to defend.

Naming one, or naming why none applies, would change how the reindustrialisation overlay
should be read.

## 9. Proposed editorial policies — adopt or reject

These were used as working defaults for the drafts above. They are **proposals**, not an
existing manifesto, and nothing on the site states them as settled policy.

**Default reader.** A financially literate reader deciding whether an account of an
Indonesian industrial or energy-transition problem deserves further attention. A practitioner
or an assessor; they do not know the biography, the earlier essays, or every institution
named. Three minutes before they decide whether to read on. The curriculum stays for readers
who want a subject in sequence.

**Voice and length.** Question, position, scope and consequence early. Observed evidence,
inference and judgment kept apart. An acronym explained where it first matters. A policy
argument names who can act, who pays, who benefits, the strongest objection, and what would
change the conclusion. Force of language is not a substitute for evidence. Most argued
essays 1 200–2 000 words; a longer technical piece earns its length through analysis the
shorter form cannot carry; brief companions 500–600; corrections and bounded updates may be
shorter than 500. Material revisions state what changed and why.

**These are editorial targets, not proof of quality**, and they have deliberately **not**
been turned into universal validators. The publish gate scales by what a piece is for, and
its floors are minimums for coherence, not quality measures.

**Success.** A reader can state the central claim, its limits and its strongest objection
after a short visit, then inspect the evidence in one completed piece. Over six months:
substantive responses engaging a specific argument, source, model or changed judgment;
corrections recorded; explicit predictions revisited. Page views, syllabus completion and
design refinements are supporting measures.

## 10. The three-reader study — PENDING, not done

No participant has been contacted and no comprehension has been measured. Nothing in this
work reports otherwise.

**Script**, for three people who do not know the work. Give them the URL and nothing else.

1. *(Before they open it)* — nothing. Do not explain the site.
2. *(After three minutes)* — "What is this person claiming?"
3. "What is the claim limited to? Where does it stop applying?"
4. "What is the strongest reason it might be wrong?"
5. "Where would you go to check it?"
6. "Is there anything here you were told is unfinished? What?"

**How to read the answers.** Question 2 failing means the entrance failed. Question 3 failing
means the scope is stated but not landing. Question 4 failing is the most important: the
objection is on the page, so if they cannot repeat it, it is not where they look. Question 6
failing is the one that matters most for honesty — if they did not notice that two of the
five reading-path steps are unwritten, the interface is not being as candid as it thinks.

**Do not brief them, do not explain, and do not defend the site during the session.** Run it
before the application deadline, not after.
